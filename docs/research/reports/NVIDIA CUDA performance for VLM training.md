# Profile first: your GPU waits on the CPU

Your 60 % utilization, a main thread pinned at 100 % of a core, and 6.9 GB of 24 GB used all point the same way. The 4090 is starved by CPU-side launch work and wasteful recomputation, not limited by its own compute. NVIDIA and PyTorch describe this symptom explicitly: at small batch "CPU overhead can become larger than GPU run time", and the Nsight timeline shows the CPU at 100 % with gaps between kernels ([PyTorch blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/accelerating-pytorch-with-cuda-graphs/)). The first lever is switching gradient checkpointing off, which is the cheapest change and the one with the strongest evidence in your numbers. The 6.9 GB peak leaves about 17 GB free, and checkpointing adds an extra forward pass. For a LoRA run with a frozen base I estimate about 6N FLOPs per token with checkpointing against 4N without (my derivation, not a published number). Next come host-side fixes: syncs, data loading, and kernel fusion via Liger or a partial torch.compile. QLoRA, DoRA, FP8, and multi-stream tricks are unlikely to help. For the custom CUDA checker, NVIDIA's guidance says the same thing in different words: per-polygon launches and copies cost more than the work, so you need one batched launch over a pinned, reused buffer. Almost every speedup figure in the sources comes from A100/H100 or from different models, so every number below is a hypothesis until it is measured on the 4090. The profiling section gives exact commands for doing that, and the last section lists what must be measured.

## Launch overhead explains the 60 % utilization, but the evidence is indirect

NVIDIA's CUDA Graph documentation breaks per-launch CPU cost into layers: **10-100 us** for Python-to-C++ transitions, marshalling, and the GIL; **5-20 us** for the framework runtime and dispatch; **5-15 us** for driver submission; and 1-5 us for hardware submission. NVIDIA says these "may vary" and gives no GPU or CUDA version ([NVIDIA DL CUDA Graph basics](https://docs.nvidia.com/dl-cuda-graph/cuda-graph-basics/introduction.html)). The same page lists the conditions where this hurts: a fast GPU paired with a slow CPU, heavy dispatch stacks, and many kernels that each run only microseconds. A 2B-parameter LoRA model at micro-batch 4 fits that description, because every linear layer carries thin r=16 LoRA matmuls plus norms, rotary, activations, and casts. The only hard published figure is a V100/CUDA 10.1 example (2019). A 2.9 us kernel cost **9.6 us** per kernel with a sync after each launch, **3.8 us** with launches overlapped, and **3.4 us** as a CUDA Graph, with about 400 us to build the graph ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/cuda-graphs/)). I found no controlled launch-latency figure for the 4090 under CUDA 12 or 13, and no per-op eager figure for a current PyTorch release. Secondary sources give 5-20 us per op and are not controlled benchmarks.

My inference, labelled as such, is that gradient checkpointing worsens the CPU bottleneck, since it makes the CPU issue roughly 1.3-1.5x as many ops per step (unsourced estimate). Rented hosts also vary in single-core CPU speed, and NVIDIA names a slow CPU paired with a modern GPU as a risk, so record the host CPU model with every run.

Streams are the wrong tool for this problem. PyTorch states that work within a stream is serialized and that with non-default streams synchronization is your responsibility ([PyTorch CUDA semantics](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.15/notes/cuda.html)). A PyTorch forum answer says that launching from several streams in one Python thread helps little because of the GIL, and that one batched kernel beats many launches across streams. That is a forum opinion, and I found no NVIDIA sentence stating it outright. The conclusion rests on NVIDIA's cost breakdown, where the CPU-side layers dominate. Streams earn their place only for overlapping pinned, `non_blocking` host-to-device copies with compute.

CUDA Graphs attack the launch cost directly, but they are hard to apply to a VLM. A replay is one `cudaGraphLaunch`. In MLPerf v1.0, graphs gave **1.70x** on Mask R-CNN and 1.12x on BERT at 4096 GPUs, and NVIDIA had to remove CPU-GPU syncs and replace dynamic-shape loss gathering with static tensors plus masks to make capture possible ([PyTorch blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/accelerating-pytorch-with-cuda-graphs/)). Capture forbids `.item()`-style syncs. Under `torch.compile`, CUDAGraph trees re-record for every distinct input shape and skip functions that mutate eager inputs ([CUDAGraph Trees docs, 2.8](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.8/torch.compiler_cudagraph_trees.html)). Variable image grids, sequence lengths, gradient checkpointing, and HF's data-dependent multimodal code (mask-based image-token placement, position ids) are all capture-hostile. I found no report of CUDA Graphs on a Qwen-VL LoRA run. The sensible order is to remove syncs with `torch.cuda.set_sync_debug_mode("warn")`, which PyTorch labels experimental and incomplete ([docs](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.15/generated/torch.cuda.set_sync_debug_mode.html)). After that, bucket sequence lengths and try partial graphing of the decoder blocks. Whole-step capture comes last, if at all.

## Training levers: turn checkpointing off first, then fuse, and skip QLoRA

**Checkpointing.** Megatron measured **30-40 %** overhead for full recomputation and reached 54.2 % MFU with selective recomputation against 42.1 % with full ([Korthikanti et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05198v1)). TRL ships `gradient_checkpointing_kwargs={"selective": True}` for SFT, which saves the attention output and "recovers most of the checkpointing slowdown", and it can be disabled outright ([TRL docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/main/en/reducing_memory_usage)). Test micro-batch 4 with no checkpointing. If it runs out of memory, try micro-batch 2 with accumulation 16, or selective checkpointing. Activation memory for this model without checkpointing is unmeasured; I guessed a few GB to 10+ GB, so the 17 GB headroom probably suffices.

**MFU baseline.** The right denominator is the dense bf16 peak of **165.2 TFLOPS** (FP32 accumulate), from the Ada whitepaper ([NVIDIA Ada whitepaper v2.1](https://images.nvidia.com/aem-dam/Solutions/Data-Center/l4/nvidia-ada-gpu-architecture-whitepaper-v2.1.pdf)). The 330 TFLOPS fp16 headline needs FP16 accumulation, which bf16 autocast does not use. My arithmetic for your run is about 17,900 tokens per step, about 5,270 tokens/s, and roughly **22 % MFU on useful FLOPs (4N) or 33 % counting recompute (6N)**. That assumes a 1.7B-parameter language model and ignores the vision tower. It is a worked example, not a measurement.

**Micro-batch and packing.** HF's padding-free FA2 collator reported up to 2x tokens/s on high-length-variance data and about 1.4x on more uniform data (8 A100s, 7-8B models) ([HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/packing-with-FA2)). Your samples are near-uniform at roughly 560 tokens with a fixed image-token block, so expect the low end or less. Measure the padding fraction first, as 1 minus the sum of lengths over batch times max length. Whether Qwen3-VL's multimodal RoPE works with flattened batches is unverified. Micro-batch 4 already gives about 2,240 tokens per forward, which is enough to saturate the big GEMMs. Going larger mainly helps launch amortization, not GEMM efficiency.

**Attention.** FlashAttention-2 supports Ada including the 4090, with bf16 and head dim up to 256. FA3 is Hopper-only and FA4 targets Hopper and Blackwell ([FlashAttention repo](https://github.com/Dao-AILab/flash-attention)). HF's own Qwen3-VL example uses SDPA ([HF docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/main/en/model_doc/qwen3_vl)). My estimate puts attention at about 5 % of FLOPs at 560 tokens, so the backend choice matters little for speed. FA2's value here would be padding-free batching. There is no 4090 benchmark of FA2 against SDPA in the sources.

**Fusion.** PyTorch reported a **43 %** average training speedup from `torch.compile` on an A100 across 163 models (51 % with AMP), and said gains are lower on desktop GPUs such as the 3090 ([PyTorch 2.0 page](https://pytorch.org/get-started/pytorch-2.0/)). HF warns against compiling the whole model outside `generate()` because of per-step recompiles ([HF](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/en/perf_train_gpu_one)). For a VLM with variable shapes, compile only the decoder layers and pad to a few buckets, which is my inference. Liger-Kernel claims about **20 % throughput and 60 % memory** gains on LLaMA-3-8B with 8 A100s, using its own measurements ([Liger README](https://github.com/linkedin/Liger-Kernel)). Its per-kernel microbenchmarks show RMSNorm about 7x and RoPE about 8x faster ([Liger paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10989)). The README lists Qwen2-VL, Qwen2.5-VL, and Qwen3 but not Qwen3-VL, so patching Qwen3-VL's text decoder is a correctness risk that needs a loss-parity test. My honest range for fusion on an already-SDPA baseline is 1.1-1.5x, and the 2x headlines compare against weaker baselines. Your 152k vocabulary makes the logits tensor about 1.4 GB in fp32 at micro-batch 4, and the lm_head is roughly 18 % of language-model FLOPs by my estimate. Fused linear cross-entropy is therefore the most relevant Liger kernel. Computing loss only on completion tokens is an untested idea. A fused optimizer is a standard option, but the PyTorch docs list fused AdamW as newer and less tested ([PyTorch optim docs](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.8/optim.html)). With LoRA's tiny trainable set, I expect little gain.

**Precision and quantization.** FP8 Tensor Cores exist on Ada at 330 TFLOPS with FP32 accumulate, double bf16 ([Ada whitepaper](https://images.nvidia.com/aem-dam/Solutions/Data-Center/l4/nvidia-ada-gpu-architecture-whitepaper-v2.1.pdf)). Transformer Engine lists FP8 training on Ada ([TransformerEngine](https://github.com/NVIDIA/TransformerEngine)). No benchmark shows an end-to-end gain at 2B LoRA scale, and with thin GEMMs the cast overhead probably eats the gain. Do not pursue it. QLoRA was **39 % slower** than bf16 LoRA on Llama-2-7B (2.79 h against 1.85 h), saving about a third of the memory ([Raschka](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/practical-tips-for-finetuning-llms)). You have no memory problem, and with checkpointing dequantization runs twice, so skip QLoRA. PEFT also says DoRA has bigger overhead than LoRA ([PEFT guide](https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/main/en/developer_guides/lora)), though I found no number.

**Data and runtime.** PyTorch recommends `pin_memory=True`, `num_workers>0`, and `zero_grad(set_to_none=True)`, and warns that `print(cuda_tensor)`, `.item()`, `.cpu()`, `nonzero()`, and Python branches on tensor values synchronize ([Tuning Guide](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/recipes/recipes/tuning_guide.html)). If image preprocessing runs in the main process, that alone could explain the pinned thread, and workers or cached pre-tokenized inputs would beat any kernel change.

## Evaluation sampling and the polygon checker follow the same batching rule

**vLLM and HF generate.** The notes contain profiling guidance for vLLM but no sources on sampling speed, and nothing on HF `generate` beyond the `torch.compile` warning. The following is therefore inference. Decode is dominated by per-token launch cost, which vLLM already mitigates with CUDA graphs. Larger batches amortize both launches and weight reads, so check that the sampling job sends enough concurrent sequences. For a VLM, check whether time sits in the CPU-side image preprocessing and vision encoder or in decode kernels. vLLM's docs recommend nsys for low overhead and warn the torch profiler is medium-overhead and not for benchmarking ([vLLM profiling docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/contributing/profiling/)). When profiling vLLM with CUDA graphs on, you need `--cuda-graph-trace=node` to see the kernels inside them. The same is true of HF generate if you graph it.

**The checker.** NVIDIA's Best Practices Guide gives the controlling rules. Minimize host-device transfers, even if that means running kernels that do not beat the CPU. Coalesce global access. Use blocks of 128-256 threads, and launch more blocks than SMs, ideally thousands ([Best Practices Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-c-best-practices-guide/index.html)). Pinned memory gives the highest bandwidth, and async copies require pinned memory and non-default streams. Batching many small transfers into one performs much better ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/how-optimize-data-transfers-cuda-cc/)). The 4090 has 128 SMs (Ada whitepaper), so a 64x64 raster of 4,096 threads is only about 32 blocks and cannot fill it.

The arithmetic points the same way. A 256x256 byte mask is 64 KB, which at about 25 GB/s pinned PCIe 4.0 (forum anecdotes, system-dependent) is roughly 2.6 us of transfer. That is smaller than the 5-20 us fixed cost per call. Per-polygon calls are therefore overhead-bound by about an order of magnitude, which matches your measured 0.002-0.19x of CPU speed. I did not read `cuda/gpucheck.cpp`, so I do not know whether it calls `cudaMalloc`/`cudaFree` per call; if it does, that is a prime suspect and costs far more than a memcpy.

The standard fixes, all inferences from the sources, are these. Concatenate all polygons into one flat buffer with offsets, then do one H2D copy, one launch with one block per polygon, one D2H copy, and one sync. Keep a persistent device arena (`cudaMallocAsync` or allocate-once) and reuse pinned staging buffers. Return per-polygon summary statistics or bit-packed masks instead of full masks when only validation results are needed. Use one thread per pixel with the edge list in shared memory, where same-address reads broadcast without bank conflicts. Write the crossing test branch-free, and reduce with `cub::BlockReduce` or warp shuffle rather than global atomics. A CUDA Graph over the build, check, and score chain is worth it only after batching, as NVIDIA's example shows 2.8x per tiny kernel. Published GPU wins are at huge scale: cuSpatial reports 140x at 1e6 points, saturating near 1,000 points ([cuSpatial](https://medium.com/rapids-ai/good-things-come-in-twos-binary-predicate-support-and-more-in-cuspatial-22-12-1d89292e581d)). I found no published break-even for many tiny polygons, so run your own sweep over batch size from 1 to 1e5 and raster size.

## How to profile each workload on a rented RTX 4090 in Docker

The container is the main uncertainty. NVIDIA's counter restriction (`ERR_NVGPUCTRPERM`) can block Nsight Compute and nsys GPU metrics, and the documented fixes are host-side `NVreg_RestrictProfilingToAdminUsers=0` or starting the container with `--cap-add=SYS_ADMIN` ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/ERR_NVGPUCTRPERM)). CPU sampling needs a host `perf_event_paranoid` of 2 or lower, and one forum report shows a privileged container still failing at paranoid 4 ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/cpu-sampling-in-privileged-docker-container-via-sudo-nsys/218361)). Vast's docs, as far as I could read them, say nothing about privileged mode or capabilities ([Vast docs](https://docs.vast.ai/documentation/instances/docker-environment)), so you probably cannot add them. Put three checks in your preflight, and plan on nsys with CPU sampling off and torch.profiler. Treat `ncu` as unavailable until proven otherwise.

```
nsys status -e
cat /proc/driver/nvidia/params | grep RmProfilingAdminOnly
cat /proc/sys/kernel/perf_event_paranoid
```

**Training.** Start with torch.profiler, which uses CUPTI through Kineto and documents no counter-privilege requirement. Wrap it in a `TrainerCallback` that calls `prof.step()` once per optimizer step. This sketch is mine and untested, though Oumi ships a similar callback ([Oumi](https://oumi.ai/docs/en/latest/_modules/oumi/core/callbacks/profiler_step_callback.html)). Keep `with_stack` off at first, since `record_shapes` and `with_stack` add overhead ([PyTorch profiler docs](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.15/profiler.html)).

```
from torch.profiler import profile, schedule, tensorboard_trace_handler, ProfilerActivity
prof = profile(activities=[ProfilerActivity.CPU, ProfilerActivity.CUDA],
  schedule=schedule(skip_first=3, wait=1, warmup=2, active=3, repeat=1),
  on_trace_ready=tensorboard_trace_handler("prof_train"), record_shapes=True)
# on_train_begin: prof.__enter__(); on_step_end: prof.step(); on_train_end: prof.__exit__(None,None,None)
print(prof.key_averages().table(sort_by="self_cuda_time_total", row_limit=25))
```

Then run nsys for the timeline. The flags are documented in the [Nsight Systems guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/nsight-systems/UserGuide/index.html), but my combination is untested. Bracket about 5 post-warmup steps with `torch.cuda.profiler.start()` and `stop()`. The `-s none` flag turns CPU sampling off, which a non-privileged container needs ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/nsight-systems-initialization-errors/181287)).

```
nsys profile -t cuda,nvtx,osrt,cudnn,cublas -s none --pytorch=autograd-nvtx \
  --capture-range=cudaProfilerApi --capture-range-end=stop -o prof_train \
  .venv/bin/python -I -m experiments.phase2.<train_script> <config>
nsys stats -r cuda_gpu_kern_sum,cuda_api_sum,cuda_kern_exec_sum,nvtx_sum,nvtx_gpu_proj_sum prof_train.nsys-rep
nsys analyze prof_train.nsys-rep
```

Reading the results is my synthesis. Gaps in the GPU row with a busy main thread and a large `cudaLaunchKernel` share mean launch-bound. Gaps after long `cudaStreamSynchronize` or `cudaMemcpy` calls mean sync-bound. Gaps while the main thread waits in DataLoader or `osrt` calls mean data-bound. Dense kernels without gaps mean compute- or memory-bound, and only then does `ncu` earn its cost. No canned nsys report gives idle percentage, so export to SQLite for that. Do not rely on `nvidia-smi` GPU-Util, which only says a kernel ran during the sample window. NVML defines it that way (via [PyTorch docs](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.13/generated/torch.cuda.utilization.html)), so it cannot separate a saturated GPU from one running tiny kernels. DCGM's `SM_ACTIVE` and `PIPE_TENSOR_ACTIVE` fields are better ([DCGM](https://docs.nvidia.com/datacenter/dcgm/latest/user-guide/feature-overview.html)), but nothing confirms they work on GeForce, and they need admin privileges and can conflict with Nsight counters. Skip DCGM on Vast.

**vLLM.** Use the current `--profiler-config` flag (needs vLLM 0.13 or later, so your 0.32 qualifies). Start and stop the profiler through the server endpoints with only a few requests in between. A second `/start_profile` before stop returns HTTP 409, and stop can take minutes ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/contributing/profiling/)). I did not verify the behavior of 0.32 specifically.

```
vllm serve <model> --profiler-config '{"profiler":"torch","torch_profiler_dir":"./vllm_profile"}'
curl -X POST localhost:8000/start_profile   # send a few requests
curl -X POST localhost:8000/stop_profile    # open rank0.*.pt.trace.json.gz in ui.perfetto.dev
# nsys variant
VLLM_WORKER_MULTIPROC_METHOD=spawn nsys profile -s none --trace-fork-before-exec=true \
  --cuda-graph-trace=node --capture-range=cudaProfilerApi --capture-range-end=repeat \
  vllm serve <model> --profiler-config.profiler cuda
```

For HF generate, use the same torch.profiler wrapper around a few `generate` calls.

**The checker.** The nsys timeline answers the main question, which is where the time goes. Time two things separately. CUDA events around the kernel only, and wall-clock around the whole nanobind call. Their difference is overhead. Warm up first, since the first launch and context creation are slower. Then, inside the nsys timeline, look for repeated `cudaMalloc`/`cudaFree`, many small `cudaMemcpy` calls, and `cudaStreamSynchronize` between tiny kernels, with GPU rows mostly empty. `ncu` only measures kernel duration, and its profiled durations will not match host timers. Use it after batching, if it works at all, on the top kernel for Launch Statistics, Occupancy, and Memory Workload sections ([Nsight Compute guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/nsight-compute/ProfilingGuide/index.html)).

## Open gaps that only the GPU can close

The sources leave these unanswered, and they decide the order of work. First, the per-op launch cost and the host CPU's single-core speed on the actual rented box, via an empty-kernel microbenchmark and the nsys API-to-start latency. Second, the real activation memory at micro-batch 4 with no checkpointing, and the step time saved. Third, the padding fraction, and whether Qwen3-VL accepts padding-free batches with its multimodal RoPE. Fourth, whether Liger has a verified Qwen3-VL patch, and whether it passes a loss-parity check. Fifth, whether `torch.compile` on the decoder layers pays on a 4090 after recompiles with your shape distribution. Sixth, FA2 against SDPA at 560 tokens, which I estimate as a small difference. Seventh, whether nsys CUDA tracing and ncu work unprivileged on Vast hosts, and whether DCGM fields exist on GeForce. Eighth, the batch-size break-even for the checker, and whether `gpucheck.cpp` allocates per call, which I did not read. Ninth, vLLM and HF-generate sampling throughput versus concurrency, where the notes contain no sources.

## Conclusion

The sources reframe the problem. Your slow jobs are mostly not "the GPU is too small" or "needs lower precision". They share one pattern, which is that fixed per-call costs on the CPU side exceed the useful work per call. That cost shows up as launch gaps in training, as per-polygon copies in the checker, and probably as small decode batches in sampling. The fixes follow the same rule too: do less per step (no recompute), issue fewer and larger operations (fusion, batching, buffer reuse), and keep the CPU ahead of the GPU (no syncs, workers, pinned memory).

The sources are weak on exactly what you run. Nearly every quantitative claim comes from A100 or H100, LLaMA-class models, or 2019 V100 launch tests, and none from a Qwen3-VL LoRA on a 4090. Treat the lever ranking as a hypothesis list. The cheap experiment, a no-checkpointing run with a 5-step torch.profiler trace, costs minutes of rental time and will confirm or kill the main hypothesis before you pay for anything harder.
